Reassess Safety Solvers, Muting Controls, and Spare Strategies Now

Published Sep 6, 2026, 6:03 AM AWST•APAC•Full category signal

Decision at a glance

Top move

New mid-tier safety logic solvers fill the gap between single-loop relays and full safety PLCs, creating a procurement choice that can lower engineering and lifecycle cost drivers if scoped correctly

Key takeaways

  • New mid-tier safety logic solvers fill the gap between single-loop relays and full safety PLCs, creating a procurement choice that can lower engineering and lifecycle cost drivers if scoped correctly.[1]
  • Practical failures in muting (temporary bypass of safety barriers) are common and often stem from installation shortcuts, which means sensor, scanner and guarding consumables plus control logic need verification before accepting vendor 'as-is' designs.[2]
  • A recent Australian plant digitalisation case shows cloud-based control plus MES integration changes demand toward standardised I/O modules, traceability hardware (barcode scanners/labels) and disciplined spare-part lists — useful when considering stocking and contract scope.[3]

Why this matters for the category manager

New mid-tier safety logic solvers fill the gap between single-loop relays and full safety PLCs, creating a procurement choice that can lower engineering and lifecycle cost drivers if scoped correctly. Practical failures in muting (temporary bypass of safety barriers) are common and often stem from installation shortcuts, which means sensor, scanner and guarding consumables plus control logic need verification before accepting vendor 'as-is' designs. A recent Australian plant digitalisation case shows cloud-based control plus MES integration changes demand toward standardised I/O modules, traceability hardware (barcode scanners/labels) and disciplined spare-part lists — useful when considering stocking and contract scope. Operationally in APAC sites with dispersed safety functions, choosing multichannel logic solvers shifts cost from engineering hours into hardware/specification decisions and spare SKUs — buyers should treat that as a procurement lever

What changed

  • Shifted observable focus from connectivity/hardened edge spares to safety-control devices (multiloop logic solvers) and guarding/muting implementation risks.
  • Added a supplier/practical example of digital control integration that implies standardized I/O and traceability consumable demand; no new vendor-bundling evidence for connectivity services since last run.

Category impact

Cost / money

  • Choosing multiloop logic solvers can reduce total engineering and documentation spend but increases SKU diversity for spares (specialised modules and certified I/O), changing working-capital needs for site stores.[1]
  • Fixing muting-related installation gaps often requires sensor/guard replacement or added sensing hardware and commissioning time, which raises short-term OPEX for sites that currently accept shortcut designs.[2]

Supplier / commercial

  • Suppliers of mid-tier safety devices can push for higher-margin packaged solutions (hardware plus lifecycle services); insist on separated line items for hardware, engineering and ongoing validation to retain negotiation leverage.[1]
  • Vendors proposing muting fixes may bundle scanners, distance sensors and configuration services; pre-qualify local integrators to avoid last-minute premium for on-site commissioning.[2]

Safety / operations

  • Adopting multiloop solvers without aligned lifecycle processes risks undocumented logic changes and unclear maintenance responsibilities — ensure firmware/configuration ownership is contractually defined to avoid safety drift.[1][3]
  • Muting misconfiguration creates real entry risks at conveyors; operational teams must validate sensor placement and timing settings against the standard rather than relying on vendor defaults.[2]
  • Cloud-based control integration increases dependency on validated I/O and traceability hardware; maintenance and spare procedures must include these devices in uptime and change-control plans.[3]

What to watch

  • Watch for suppliers narrowing quote validity or bundling configuration/validation services into hardware prices for safety devices — this reduces price comparability and can lock buyers into higher lifecycle costs.[1]
  • Watch whether site teams are using pallets or simple triggers as muting inputs — that practice is explicitly discouraged by IEC 62046 and creates a latent safety and compliance liability.[2]

Muting in practice: when safety becomes a vulnerability

Signal moderateSource-grounded
Source notes [2]Read source

What happened

The article explains that muting — temporarily bypassing safety barriers to allow material flow — is often implemented poorly and can create real safety gaps. It cites IEC 62046 requirements and practical hazards like using pallets as muting triggers or gaps from partially loaded pallets; remedies include smart process gating and dynamic format guarding. Buyers should verify that proposed muting solutions meet the standard and avoid quick fixes that increase liability

Why the category manager should care

Treat muting fixes as combined hardware+configuration procurements that need performance acceptance criteria tied to IEC 62046

Key facts

  • IEC 62046 requires at least two independent bridging signals for muting
  • Practical hazards include pallet-trigger misuse and clearance gaps next to goods

Unconscious risks Various types of muting are used in industrial automation: whether they be 2-sensor, 4-sensor, time-controlled or sequence-controlled. The IEC 62046 international standard1 regulates the requirements for entry and exit stations with muting and stipulates in particular that: muting must be activated via at least two independent bridging signals muting must offer protection against foreseeable incorrect operation or manipulation
For operators, this means an unconsciously increased liability risk and potentially serious consequences for employee safety. Hazard 1: Safety gap due to pallet muting In automated systems, meshed containers or other objects that are difficult for muting sensors to detect are often fed onto or discharged from pallets (Figure 1)
Designed to allow objects to pass through safety barriers in factories, muting can also introduce new safety risks if not implemented correctly. Muting allows the safety function on machines or systems to be temporarily bypassed so that material can be fed in or out through protective devices such as light curtains or laser scanners without interrupting the production process
